Bloat Can't Wait: What Every Great Pyrenees Owner Should Know About GDV

A tight belly and unproductive retching are an emergency. Here's what bloat looks like in a Pyr, what to do, and what may lower the risk.

← All Field Notes

A Great Pyrenees can look as calm as a cloud and still be in trouble. Bloat, or gastric dilatation-volvulus (GDV), is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening emergency. The stomach fills with air, food, or fluid, swells, and can twist on itself. That twist cuts off blood flow to the stomach and spleen, presses on the heart and lungs, and will not wait. The American Kennel Club says the breed is susceptible. Large, deep-chested dogs are the ones most at risk.

It can follow a big meal, and it can also happen when the stomach looks empty. You cannot tell from the doorway whether the stomach has only swollen or has already twisted. Either way, it is an emergency.

Know these signs

Watch for some, not necessarily all, of these:

  • Retching with nothing coming up. Gagging or trying to vomit and bringing up little or nothing is a classic warning.
  • Heavy drooling.
  • A swollen or tight belly. A heavy coat can hide a tight belly, so look under the fur rather than judging from the outline alone.
  • Restlessness. Pacing, an anxious look, or staring at the abdomen.
  • Odd posture. Standing and stretching, or a “praying” position with the chest low and the front legs stretched forward.
  • Panting, pale gums, weakness, or collapse.

Early signs can be subtle. The AKC Canine Health Foundation notes that a bloated dog does not always have a visibly distended belly. If your Pyr can't get comfortable, especially after a meal, don't wait for a textbook picture.

Go now

This is right-now veterinary territory, not a wait-and-see problem. Call your veterinarian or the nearest emergency clinic, tell them you are on the way with a possible bloat, and go. Don't offer food, water, or home remedies, and don't try to treat it yourself.

Without medical and surgical care, GDV is fatal. Cornell's canine health center reports that more than 80 percent of dogs survive when they receive that care, and veterinary surgeons warn that the odds worsen the longer treatment is delayed. At the clinic, the team will stabilize your dog, confirm the diagnosis, usually with X-rays, and, if the stomach has twisted, operate to untwist it and often tack it in place.

What may lower the risk

No single habit can prevent bloat, and the causes are not fully understood. Veterinarians still recommend a few practical steps:

  • Split the meals. Feed two or three smaller meals instead of one large one.
  • Discourage gulping. A dog that bolts food is at higher risk.
  • Ease up after eating. Skip hard running and rough play right after a meal.
  • Leave the bowl on the floor. Raised bowls have been linked with higher risk, so skip them unless your veterinarian has a reason.
  • Ask about a gastropexy. This surgery tacks the stomach to the body wall, often when a dog is already under for a spay or neuter. It greatly reduces the chance of a life-threatening twist. It does not stop the stomach from swelling.

Keep the emergency clinic number where you can find it at night. If you even suspect bloat, go. In a Great Pyrenees, bloat can't wait.
